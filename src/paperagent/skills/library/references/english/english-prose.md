# Meaning-preserving English academic prose

First distinguish grammar correction, substantive revision, compression and translation. Preserve scientific meaning, evidence, numbers and confirmed terminology. Do not require a whole-paper review to repair a sentence.

Use concrete subjects and direct verbs. Make actors, comparisons and referents explicit: higher than what, which quantity, measured in whom, under which condition? Introduce necessary background before dependent concepts. Expanding an acronym is not always enough to explain its role.

Unpack dense noun chains by their actual meaning using a possessive, preposition or clause. Preserve conventional technical terms. “Willingness to buy” is not “purchases”; “welfare” is not a cosmetic synonym for “wellness”. Do not guess an ambiguous concept during translation; preserve it or identify the uncertainty.

Use one term per defined concept, with author-approved variants and abbreviations. Read confirmed manuscript terminology; proposed names remain suggestions. Preserve legitimate repetition in Methods and matched comparisons. Avoid rotating synonyms merely to make the language appear varied.

Use tense and voice by function. Completed procedures often use past tense; statements about a figure or current interpretation may use present tense. Neither all-passive nor all-active is a universal rule. Follow an explicitly selected English variant consistently.

For paragraph flow, identify the relation between sentences and paragraphs. Use familiar information to introduce new information. Replace an empty transition with the missing relationship where appropriate; do not simply rotate “Moreover” into “Furthermore”. Avoid repeated “We first / We then / We next” frames when they obscure an otherwise clear procedure, but retain necessary sequence.

Compress repeated functions before deleting scientific content. Preserve comparators, uncertainty, citations and inference limits. Shorter wording is worse if it changes the claim. Read the exact candidate and available neighboring text after editing.

For citations, distinguish bibliographic identity, formatting and claim-level support. A DOI lookup or saved reference proves neither that the source supports the adjacent assertion nor that a secondary citation describes the primary study correctly. Verify at the level needed for the changed claim. If the source is unavailable, retain that limitation.

Stock phrases, repeated openings, vague subjects and excessive dash density are contextual diagnostics. Do not globally ban common words, strip valid technical hyphens or infer AI authorship. A literal scan cannot judge naturalness or scientific validity.

Before handing over substantial prose, compare original and revised meaning, numbers, citations, term choices and qualifiers. State changes to meaning separately from language edits. For a grammar-only request, keep the response concise and avoid pretending to have verified the underlying science.
